§ 1 — THE ANSWERWhat actually changed

The model is being marked against a different kind of thing than it used to be, and the switch has a date: commit c6b5342, 1 September 2026 — “Fly Mehta’s identified field”.

Before that commit the project had never once compared itself to a measured atmosphere. It compared itself to closed-form mathematics, to the same NASA document its own derivatives were read out of, and to a second flight-dynamics code fed identical coefficients. Those comparisons return small numbers because of what they ask.

A 0.4% Dutch roll frequency is the answer to “was CR-2144 transcribed and unprimed correctly?” A 32% load shortfall is the answer to “does a rigid 747 reproduce a DC-10’s flight-data-recorder trace?” They were never the same measurement, and only the second one is about aeroplanes.

§ 3 — MECHANISMFour reasons the published numbers drifted the wrong way

None of them is a defect. Three are the record getting more honest; one is real physics, and it is the oldest assumption in the register.

  1. The model was flown away from where its data was linearised

    This is the largest identified physical cause. aero.py carries Mach into wave_drag and the thrust ram term and nowhere else — there is no Prandtl–Glauert factor on C_Lα, C_mα or anything else. The derivatives are frozen at M 0.80 / 40,000 ft, and the project started flying elsewhere.

    The price is measured at two points and they agree about the shape of the problem. Both halves of the fix turned out to be already in hand — see the callout below the table.

  2. The record started reporting the rows it had been omitting

    Session 16 grew the 747 mode table from two of CR-2144’s four published longitudinal factors to all four, adding rows at +14.4% and −1.4%. No derivative moved: 42 of 44 scalars were bit-identical across the pass, compared as hex representations against the tree extracted at the preceding commit. The table’s worst case grew because two rows had been missing.

  3. Two errors that had been cancelling were separated

    The JSBSim short period read 0.04% → 3.95% → 1.30% → 0.04% across session 18. Only the last is honest — the first was an α̇-contaminated C_mα of −1.0637 standing in for a coupling the model did not have. Same number, opposite meaning. In the same pass five fidelity improvements made the phugoid worse, 3.33% → 6.58%, before session 19 localised 96% of that to a single entry of the 4×4 — M_u, a Mach-tuck term a constant-coefficient model structurally cannot carry.

  4. A source correction moved the headline load downward

    Session 26 obtained Parks et al. 1985 and found the project had been flying a hybrid vortex: one paper’s 500 ft core radius with another’s 85 ft/s strength. Parks’ own triple is 600 ft / 85 ft/s / 3500 ft, and dw/dx = V₀/r₀, so a wider core at fixed strength is a gentler gradient. Cost: −4.26% of the headline peak-to-peak, taking the comparison from 70.3% to 67.3% of the record. A correction that makes your own result worse is the one kind that cannot have been chosen for convenience.

Both halves of the fix were already in the building

The sourced curve. ASSUMPTIONS.md C3 has called the Mach axis unbounded since session 12, and the plan declined the bound repeatedly because it “needed chart reads off a poor scan”. Reading refs/NASA-CR-2144.pdf’s own text layer this session: printed pages 220–222 carry CL_α(M), CD_α(M), Cm_α(M), Cm_α̇(M), Cm_q(M) and CL_M / CD_M / Cm_M — all captioned B-747 / 636600 lb / .25 c̄ / Flexible, all against a Mach axis running 0 to 1.0, with SL / 20,000 ft / 40,000 ft curves each. The LES condition is M 0.406; the axis starts at M 0. The 40,000 ft curve is the one the model was linearised on, so one sheet gives both the value in use and the value at the excursion. Page 222 is, by name, the “Mach content of the speed derivatives” the record says is missing from the phugoid.

The implementation. A branch one commit ahead of main and never merged — dated 26 August — already carried a Prandtl–Glauert factor across the whole longitudinal lift-slope family, g(z), the geopotential-altitude conversion and Mach-scheduled control derivatives. Three later sessions wrote as though it did not exist, and one of them spent its headline effort pricing the Mach axis with a hand-rolled PG factor that was already in the repository. It is now rebased and merged, at 821 passed / 1 skipped, after three pins were re-captured against the new gravity and atmosphere.

Do not over-claim this on the day. The merge is a gravity-and-atmosphere change, not yet a compressibility one. pg_mach_ref defaults negative and no registry entry declares one, so Prandtl–Glauert is present and inert and the LES ratio has not moved. What changed is that the Mach axis went from source-gated to one field-assignment away, with the curve to assign from sitting on a page the project has held since session 1.

What the merge did move: three of the four 747 modes improved against CR-2144 Table IX-5 with nothing aimed at it. The fourth, phugoid ωn, moved −0.398% against gravity’s −0.3817% — Lanchester’s 1:1 — which is the right answer moving away from a reference the model has no form for.

Prandtl–Glauert puts the M 0.80 lift slope 1.521× too large at M 0.406, and gust load goes linearly as C_Lα. The measured LES ratio on the two domains that resolve the turbulence is 1.427 and 1.420. Session 27 ran the falsification test and it cut the claim in half rather than confirming it: correcting the slope closes 47–68% of the excess, not all of it — and correcting compressibility more completely makes agreement worse, because the airframe’s resonance moves down into more energetic turbulence.

§ 4 — DIRECT ANSWERStrip loading: used, helpful, or neither?

Measured rather than recalled. It is not on any path that produces a published number, it has moved a reported figure once in fourteen sessions, and it is still worth keeping.

integrate.step defaults to load_model=None; the shipped gust treatment is a point sample plus an analytic tangent gradient. The strip path is opt-in at three call sites only — vortex.py --strip, cat_bounds.py, lateral.py — and a test deliberately asserts that the strip and tangent estimators disagree at the vortex core edge, which is what proves the default has not been quietly switched.

So the strip increment’s isolated contribution is +22.9% of peak bank and −0.5% of the longitudinal load increment. Everything the project claims is longitudinal. Three things must be said together about that 22.9%, and only the first is favourable:

Its kernel is genuinely well verified

wind.strip_roll_moment agrees with the tabulated Clp for a rigid roll rate to 1e-3, with Stengel eq. 3.4-40’s closed form for a rectangular wing to 1e-6, with the elliptic identity ∫y²c dy = c₀b³π/64 to 1e-5, and with the equivalent-rate treatment for a linear gradient to 1e-6. It is roll only, deliberately — there is no validated pitch or yaw integral, and those channels are held at exact zero rather than filled with something plausible.

The quadrature computing it is 17% off its own calibration

At the shipped nine stations the strip integral returns 82.6% of the Clp it is calibrated against, converging at order 1.50 — about 56 stations would be needed for 1%. The loading shape underneath is a declared modelling choice carrying a 2.6% spread across defensible shapes.

The channel it moves is validated against nothing

No source held by the project records a lateral CAT response; Wingrove & Bach 1994 says so in as many words. Every bank angle above is a capability demonstration.

Verdict: keep it, and quote it for the negative result rather than the positive one. Point-versus-strip on the headline field is 0.000000 m — which is precisely what lets the record exclude “gust resolution across the span” from the 32% load shortfall. Without the strip path that exclusion would be an argument; with it, it is a number. What it must never be used for is a lateral load claim.

§ 6 — FOR THE TALKWhat to claim, and what not to

Lead with the tier ladder, not with a percentage. An audience that hears “0.4%” and then “32%” in the same talk will conclude something broke. An audience that is shown the four reference classes first will hear the 32% as the project’s first honest engagement with measured atmosphere — which is what it is.

Quote 68% for the headline, and say which run it is. Seven different percentages exist for that one comparison — 68.1% headline, 67.1% translational-only for JSBSim parity, 74.1% full configuration, 74.6% JSBSim, 72.7%, 70.1%, 76.0% — and they differ because the runs differ, not because the answer moved. Quoting two of them from different slides manufactures a regression that does not exist.

The strongest single result is not the percentage. Neither engine reaches the record: JSBSim gets 74.6% and AtiSim 67.1% on the same field, the same aeroplane and the same starting state. That excludes the solver as the explanation by measurement rather than by inference. Alongside it, the two codes agree on the LES resolution scaling to 0.5% while their absolute levels differ by 42% — comparative-not-absolute, demonstrated against an independent code on a field that was not identified from the accelerations it is asked to predict.

Three claims a listener could reasonably infer and should not.

Structural fidelity. The derivatives are a flexible airframe’s; the solver integrates a rigid body. Never assert it.
Behaviour away from trim. Derivatives are frozen and the Mach axis is unbounded. Quote the excursion with every result.
Lateral behaviour from the lateral modes. A 0.4% Dutch roll is an eigenvalue, not a gust response.

If they ask “so what would you do next?” — one digitisation of CR-2144 printed p. 220, and assign the result to pg_mach_ref. It needs no acquisition, no correspondence and no new method: the reading pipeline has already been run on the neighbouring sheet twice, reproducing itself to RMS 0.0052. That closes the model’s largest named assumption with a sourced curve rather than a textbook factor.

If one line has to carry it: the engine is validated as a comparative and mechanistic tool for the longitudinal gust response of a rigid transport at cruise — it answers which encounter is worse, how the load scales with airspeed, and why — and the 20–40% figures are the honest cost of having started to ask a harder question six days ago.
